\section{Introduction}

Razborov's flag algebra~\cite{Razborov2007} is a powerful framework for
reasoning about asymptotic properties of combinatorial structures, especially
those arising in extremal graph theory. Since its introduction, it has been successfully
applied to solve or make notable progress on several open problems, such as the minimum triangle density
problem~\cite{Razborov2008}, Erd\H{o}s's pentagon problem~\cite{HatamiHKNR13,Grzesik12},
the induced 5-cycle density problem~\cite{BaloghHLP16}, Tur\'an's tetrahedron problem~\cite{Razborov10,BaberT12}, the rainbow-triangle density problem~\cite{BaloghHLPVY17}, and
the minimum quasirandom-forcing permutation-set problem~\cite{Crudele2023six}.
 Moreover, powerful reasoning strategies in the flag algebra framework have been
automated and implemented in tools. In particular, the
{\sc Flagmatic} tool \cite{Falgas-RavryV13,PikhurkoST19} is behind many successful applications of flag algebra, including those just mentioned.

This article is a survey of Razborov's flag algebra targeted at readers with a background in theoretical computer science, particularly those working on logic, programming languages, and formal methods. While there are already excellent survey articles on the flag algebra framework \cite{Razborov13,SilvaFS16}, they tend to emphasise the algebraic aspect of the framework and be written for mathematicians. In contrast, we focus on its logical aspect and present the syntax, semantics, and reasoning principles of the framework in a manner similar to formal logic systems.

Before starting, we fix some notation and terminology used throughout the paper.
For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, we write $[n]$ for the set $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$. For a set $X$ and a nonnegative integer $n$, we write $\binom{X}{2}$ to mean the set of all 2-element subsets of $X$. Throughout the paper, we are concerned with finite simple undirected graphs $G$ whose vertex set is a subset of $\mathbb{N}$, and call them simply as graphs. We use $\mathcal{G}$ to mean the set of all such graphs. For a graph $G$, we let $V(G)$ and $E(G)$ be the vertex set and the edge set of $G$, respectively, and write $v(G)$ and $e(G)$ for their cardinalities. For a set $U \subseteq V(G)$, we define $G[U]$ to be the subgraph of $G$ induced by $U$. We use the standard notation for well-known graphs. We write $K_n$ for the complete graph with the vertex set $[n]$, and $K_{n,m}$ for the complete bipartite graph that has the vertex partition $(\{1,2,\ldots,n\},\{n+1,\ldots,n+m\})$. The $C_n$ means the cycle graph with $n$ vertices, i.e., $V(C_n) = [n]$ and $E(C_n) = \{\{i,i+1\} \,:\ i \in [n-1]\} \cup \{\{1,n\}\}$, and the $P_n$ is the path graph with $n$ vertices, i.e., $V(P_n) = [n]$ and $E(P_n) = \{\{i,i+1\} \,:\ i \in [n-1]\}$.

\section{Induced Subgraph Density, Limiting Graphs, and Extremal Problems}

Let $H$ and $G$ be graphs. The most important concept in flag algebra is the \emph{induced subgraph density} of $H$ in $G$ or simply \emph{$H$-density} in $G$. It is defined as
\[
p(H, G) \defeq \mathbb{P}\Big[G[\mathbf{U}] \simeq H\Big]
\]
where $\mathbf{U}$ is a uniformly random subset of $V(G)$ with size $v(H)$, and $\simeq$ denotes the graph isomorphism. For instance, if $H = K_2$ and $G = K_3$, then $p(H,G) = 1$ since every induced subgraph of $G$ with two vertices is isomorphic to $H$. As another example, if $H = K_2$ and $G = P_3$, then $p(H,G) = \frac{2}{3}$ since there are three induced subgraphs of $G$ with two vertices, two of which are isomorphic to $H$.

In flag algebra, we analyse the properties of graphs using the density function $p$. The target graph $G$ of this analysis goes to the second parameter of $p$, which we call 
\emph{host graph}. The first parameter $H$ of $p$, on the other hand, is a part of property speficiation, and describes a pattern to be searched for in $G$. We call $H$ \emph{pattern graph}. Usually, $G$ is large, $H$ is small, and $p(H,G)$ describes a local property of $G$.

Note that the density function $p$ induces the representation of a graph $G$ as an infinite-dimensional vector in $[0,1]^{\mathcal{G}}$ indexed by all graphs in $\mathcal{G}$. This representation is injective up to graph isomorphism, because $p(\_,G) = p(\_,G')$ is equivalent to $G \simeq G'$. It also lets us use the product topology on $[0,1]^{\mathcal{G}}$ to reason about asymptotic properties of graph sequences, as we explain next.

Let $(G_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence of graphs. The sequence is \emph{increasing} if $v(G_n) < v(G_{n+1})$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. It is \emph{convergent} if the sequence is increasing and $\lim_{n \to \infty} p(H, G_n)$ exists for every graph $H$. Note that the latter condition means that the sequence of vector representations $(p(\_,G_n))_n$ of $(G_n)_n$ converges pointwise 
in $[0,1]^{\mathcal{G}}$. The reader might be worried that this definition of convergence is too strong, as it requires the existence of the limit for every graph $H$, so that only a handful of increasing graph sequences converge. We reassure such a reader that convergence actually occurs commonly; every increasing sequence of graphs has a convergent subsequence. The reason for this is that the pointwise convergence of vector representations of graphs comes from the product topology on $[0,1]^{\mathcal{G}}$ that is compact by Tychonoff's theorem.

We call a function $\phi$ from the set of all graphs to $[0,1]$ a \emph{limiting graph} if there exists a convergent sequence of graphs $(G_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ such that $\phi(H) = \lim_{n \to \infty} p(H, G_n)$ for every graph $H$. We write 
$\Phi$ for the set of all limiting graphs. A good mental picture (which can be formalised in terms of graphons) is that a limiting graph $\phi$ is the infinite-dimensional vector representing an undirected graph with a continuum number of vertices, where the component of $\phi$ at a pattern graph $H$ is the density of $H$ in this infinitely-large host graph. We point out that every convergent sequence $(G_n)_n$ induces a unique limiting graph $\phi$, but a limiting graph $\phi$ can be induced by multiple convergent sequences.

Using limiting graphs, we can express asymptotic extremal graph theory problems in a clean manner. A representative example is an asymptotic and induced-graph version of the Tur\'an-type problem where we want to determine the maximum possible density of a graph $H$ in large host graphs that avoid certain forbidden graphs in $\mathcal{F}$. More formally, for a graph $H$ and a finite set of graphs $\mathcal{F}$, the problem asks for the solution of the following asymptotic optimisation problem:
\[
T(H, \mathcal{F}) \defeq
\lim_{n \to \infty} \max \{ p(H, G) \,:\, v(G) = n \text{ and } p(F,G) = 0 \text{ for all } F \in \mathcal{F} \}.
\]
The solution $T(H,\mathcal{F})$ can be reformulated in terms of limiting graphs as follows:
\[
T(H, \mathcal{F}) =
\max \{ \phi(H) \,:\, \phi \in \Phi \text{ and } \phi(F) = 0 \text{ for all } F \in \mathcal{F} \}.
\]
When $H = K_2$ and $\mathcal{F} = \{K_3\}$, the answer is known to $1/2$ by the famous Mantel's theorem \hy{Citation}. More generally, when $H = K_2$ and $\mathcal{F} = \{K_{r+1}\}$ for an integer $r \geq 2$, the answer is known to be $1 - \frac{1}{r}$ by the well-known Tur\'an's theorem \hy{Citation}. 
Another example is the Ramsey multiplicity problem, which is a counting version of the famous problem on Ramsey numbers $R(s,t)$. For integers $s,t \geq 2$, the problem is to answer the next asymptotic optimisation problem: 
\[
R(s,t) \defeq
\lim_{n \to \infty} \min \{ p(K_s, G) + p(K_t, \overline{G}) \,:\, v(G) = n \},
\]
where $\overline{G}$ is the complement graph of $G$ (i.e., $V(\overline(G)) = V(G)$ and $E(\overline{G}) = \binom{V(G)}{2} \setminus E(G)$). Using limiting graphs, we can reformulate this problem as the next optimising problem over limiting graphs:
\[
R(s,t) =
\min \{ \phi(K_s) + \phi(\overline{K_t}) \,:\, \phi \in \Phi \}.
\]

A main message of this survey is that the flag algebra framework is a logic for reasoning about limiting graphs in $\Phi$, in particular, the one designed for answering questions on asymptotic extremal problems. For instance, the framework provides a syntax for expressing  bounds 
\[
T(H, \mathcal{F}) \leq c
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
R(s,t) \geq c'
\]
for some $c,c' \in \mathbb{R}$ (which require reasoning about all limiting graphs in $\Phi$),
and rules for proving the above bounds, some of which can even be automated.
In the next two sections, we elaborate this message by presenting the syntax and semantics of this logic, and the reasoning principles it supports.

\section{Syntax and Semantics of Flag Algebra}
The syntax of flag algebra consists of \emph{density expressions} $E$ and \emph{assertions} $A$. Density expressions generalise pattern graphs $H$, and define maps from limiting graphs to real numbers. Such maps correspond to features in machine learning, and describe properties of limiting graphs quantitatively. Assertions express qualitiative properties of limiting graphs using density expressions and logical connectives.

Formally, the syntax is defined as follows:
\begin{align*}
\text{Density Expressions}\quad E & ::= H \,\mid\, r \cdot E \,\mid\, 0 \,\mid\, E + E \,\mid\, 1 \,\mid\, E \cdot E
\\
\text{Assertions}\quad A & ::= \lfalse \,\mid\, \ltrue \,\mid\, E \geq E \,\mid\, \neg A \,\mid\, A \lor A 
\end{align*}
where $H$ is a graph, and $r \in \mathbb{R}$. We denote the set of all density expressions by $\Expr$, and that of assertions by $\Assn$.  Note that density expressions are built from pattern graphs using real scalar multiplication, addition, multiplication, and the constants $0$ and $1$. Assertions are constructed from the boolean constants $\lfalse$ and $\ltrue$, comparisons between density expressions, negation, and disjunction. The negation and disjunction here have the usual meanings from classical logic. As a result, they induce other logical connectives of classical logic such as conjunction and implication in the standard way:
\[
A \land A' \defeq \neg (\neg A \lor \neg A')
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
A \implies A' \defeq \neg A \lor A'.
\]
We also note that usual comparison operators between density expressions such as $=$, $>$, $\leq$, and $<$ can be defined using $\geq$ and the logical connectives in the standard way. For instance,
\[
(E_1 = E_1) \defeq (E_1 \geq E_2) \land (E_2 \geq E_1)
\quad\text{and}\quad
(E_1 < E_2) \defeq \neg (E_1 \geq E_2).
\]
Finally, we write $-E$ for $(-1) \cdot E$ and $E_1 - E_2$ for $E_1 + (-E_2)$.

The formal semantics of density expressions $E$ and assertions $A$ has the form:
\[
\db{E} : \Phi \to \mathbb{R} 
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\db{A} : \Phi \to \mathbb{B}
\]
where $\mathbb{B}$ is the set of boolean values $\{\strue,\sfalse\}$.
It is defined by induction on the structure of $E$ and $A$ using the ordered commutative algebra structure of $\mathbb{R}$ and the boolean algebra structure of $\mathbb{B}$: for a limiting graph $\phi \in \Phi$,
\begin{align*}
\db{H}\phi & \defeq \phi(H),
&
\db{r \cdot E}\phi & \defeq r \cdot
\db{E}\phi,
\\
\db{0}\phi & \defeq 0,
&
\db{E_1 + E_2}\phi & \defeq
\db{E_1}\phi + \db{E_2}\phi,
\\
\db{1}\phi & \defeq 1,
&
\db{E_1 \cdot E_2}\phi & \defeq
\db{E_1}\phi \cdot \db{E_2}\phi,
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
\db{\lfalse}\phi & \defeq \sfalse,
&
\db{\ltrue}\phi & \defeq \strue,
\\
\db{E_1 \geq E_2}\phi & \defeq
\begin{cases}
    \strue & \text{if } \db{E_1}\phi \geq \db{E_2}\phi, 
    \\ 
    \sfalse & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
&
\db{\neg A}\phi & \defeq
\begin{cases}
    \strue & \text{if } \db{A}\phi = \sfalse, 
    \\ 
    \sfalse & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}
\\
\db{A_1 \lor A_2}\phi & \defeq 
\begin{cases}
    \strue & \text{if } \db{A_1}\phi = \strue \text{ or } \db{A_2}\phi = \strue, 
    \\ 
    \sfalse & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{align*}
We say that an assertion $A$ is \emph{valid} if $\db{A}\phi = \strue$ for every limiting graph $\phi \in \Phi$,
and that it is \emph{satisfiable} if $\db{A}\phi = \strue$ for some limiting graph $\phi \in \Phi$.

Note that the syntax and semantics of flag algebra are just those of arithmetic expressions and assertions (or formulas) in the quantifier-free fragment of standard first-order logic. Graphs $H$ in density expressions correspond to real-valued variables, density expressions to arithmetic expressions over these variables, and assertions to logical formulas over these arithmetic expressions. As a result, many reasoning principles from first-order logic carry over to flag algebra directly.
For instance, the following assertions hold for all limiting graphs $\phi \in \Phi$:
\begin{align*}
  & H_1 \cdot (H_3 + H_2) + (4 \cdot H_1) \cdot H_3 = H_1 \cdot H_2 + 5 \cdot (H_1 \cdot H_3), 
  \\
  & (H_1 + H_2 \geq 0) \wedge (0 \geq H_2 - H_3) \implies H_1 + H_3 \geq 0.
\end{align*}

\begin{lem}
  \label{lemma:basic-algebra}
    The rules of ordered commutative algebras form valid equations for density expressions. Also, the rules for boolean algebras are valid assertions in flag algebra.
\end{lem}

What is then special about flag algebra in comparison with first-order logic? The answer comes from the fact that variables (i.e., graphs $H$) in flag algebra cannot be set arbitrarily, but their values must come from a single limiting graph so that they are correlated. One simple consequence of this fact is that two different variables representing isomorphic graphs always have the same value.
\begin{lem}
  \label{lemma:isomorphism}
    If graphs $H$ and $H'$ are isomorphic, the equation $H = H'$ is valid.
\end{lem}
The next useful consequences follow from the connection between a limiting graph $\phi$ and probability theory. For each graph $H$, the value $\phi(H)$ intuitively denotes the probability that, if we choose a subset of $v(H)$ vertices uniformly at random in the infinite graph represented by $\phi$, the induced subgraph on the chosen vertices is isomorphic to $H$. From this intuition, we see that $\phi(H) \in [0,1]$ and that the sum of $\phi(H')$ over all graphs $H'$ on a fixed vertex set $[n]$ equals $1$. For a finite subset $V$ of $\mathbb{N}$, we say that a set of graphs $\mathcal{H}_V$ is a \emph{complete set of graphs on $V$} if every graph on the vertex set $V$ is isomorphic to exactly one graph in $\mathcal{H}_V$. Using this terminology, 
we restate the above observations formally in the following lemmas.
\begin{lem}
  \label{lemma:non-negativity}
    The inequality $H \geq 0$ is valid for every graph $H$.
\end{lem}
\begin{lem}
  \label{lemma:sum-to-one}
    Let $V$ be a finite subset of $\mathbb{N}$, and $\mathcal{H}_V$ be a complete set of graphs 
    on $V$. Then,  
    \[
    \sum_{H \in \mathcal{H}_V} H = 1
    \] 
    is valid.
\end{lem}
Finally, we note two further consequences, which are heavily used in the applications of flag algebra. They say that the density expression $H$ for a graph $H$ can be written as a linear combination of larger graphs, and that the multiplication in density expressions and the multiplicative unit $1$ can be elimiated. 
\begin{lem}
  \label{lemma:decomposition}
    Let $V$ be a finite subset of $\mathbb{N}$, and $\mathcal{H}_V$ be a complete set of graphs 
    on $V$. Then, for every graph $G$ with $v(G) \leq |V|$, 
    \[
    G = \sum_{H \in \mathcal{H}_V} p(G,H) \cdot H
    \]
    is valid.
\end{lem}
\begin{lem}
  \label{lemma:eliminate-multiplication}
    Let $V$ be a finite subset of $\mathbb{N}$, and $\mathcal{H}_V$ be a complete set of graphs 
    on $V$. Then, for every pair of graphs $H_1$ and $H_2$ with $v(H_1) + v(H_2) \leq |V|$, there exist $r_H$ for each $H \in \mathcal{H}_V$ such that
    \[
    H_1 \cdot H_2 = \sum_{H \in \mathcal{H}_V} r_H \cdot H
    \]
    is valid. Also, if $\emptygraph$ is the graph with the empty vertex set, then
    \[ 
    1 = \emptygraph.
    \]
\end{lem}

We now illustrate the syntax and semantics of flag algebra by expressing asymptotic extremal problems mentioned in the previous section. The 
Tur\'an-type problem for a graph $H$ and a finite set of forbidden graphs $\mathcal{F}$ is to find a tightest $c \in \mathbb{R}$ such that the implication
\begin{equation}
  \label{eqn:turan-assertion}
\left(\bigwedge_{F \in \mathcal{F}} F = 0 \right) \implies H \leq c.
\end{equation}
holds for all limiting graphs $\phi$ in flag algebra. The condition $\bigwedge_{F \in \mathcal{F}} F = 0$ here expresses that a limiting graph $\phi$ avoids all the forbidden graphs in $\mathcal{F}$, and the conclusion $H \leq c$ states that the density of $H$ in $\phi$ is at most $c$.
Regarding the Ramsey multiplicity problem, we are given integers $s,t \geq 2$, and asked to find a tightest $c' \in \mathbb{R}$ that makes the following inequality valid:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eqn:ramsey-assertion}
I_s + K_t \geq c'
\end{equation}
We remind the reader that $I_s$ here is the edgeless graph with $s$ vertices.

How does flag algebra help us in solving the problems of finding tightest $c$ and $c'$ in \Cref{eqn:turan-assertion,eqn:ramsey-assertion}? The answer lies in reasoning principles of flag algebra that allow us to derive valid assertions. We give a glimpse of this aspect of flag algebra with two examples here and elaborate it in the next section.
Consider the Tur\'an-type problem with $H = K_2$ and $\mathcal{F} = \{K_3\}$, which intuitively asks us to find the best upper bound on the asymptotic edge density of triangle-free graphs. In terms of flag algebra, the problem asks us to find a tightest $c$ that makes the below implication hold for all limiting graphs:
\[
K_3 = 0 \implies K_2 \leq c
\]
The tightest $c$ is $1/2$, a well-known result called Mantel's theorem \hy{Citation}. We prove the validity of the above implication for $c = 1/2$. We start by noting the validity of the following two assertions:
\begin{align}
  K_2 & = 0 \cdot \Ithree + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Ethree + \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \Pthree + 1 \cdot \Kthree,
  \label{eqn:mantel-density-decomposition}
  \\[2ex]
  0 & \leq 1 \cdot \Ithree - \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Ethree - \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Pthree + 1 \cdot \Kthree.
  \label{eqn:mantel-key-inequality}
\end{align}
The first assertion is a consequence of \Cref{lemma:decomposition}, and the second can be proved using reasoning principles of flag algebra explained in the next section. Using these assertions and the assumption $K_3 = 0$, we derive the upper bound $1/2$ on $K_2$. The derivation is given below, where each step holds for all limiting graphs $\phi \in \Phi$ satisfying $\phi(K_3) = 0$:
\begin{align*}
  K_2 
  & = 0 \cdot \Ithree + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Ethree + \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \Pthree + 1 \cdot \Kthree
  && \text{by \Cref{eqn:mantel-density-decomposition}}
  \\
  & \leq \left(0 \cdot \Ithree + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Ethree + \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \Pthree + 1 \cdot \Kthree \right) 
  + 
  \left(
  \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Ithree - \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot \Ethree - \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot \Pthree
+ \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Kthree\right)
  && \text{by \Cref{eqn:mantel-key-inequality}}
  \\
  & = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Ithree + \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot \Ethree + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Pthree + \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot \Kthree
  && \text{by \Cref{lemma:basic-algebra}}
  \\
  & = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Ithree + \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot \Ethree + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Pthree  + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Kthree
  && \text{since } K_3 = 0 
  \\
  & \leq \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Ithree + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Ethree + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Pthree + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \Kthree
  && 
  \text{by \Cref{lemma:non-negativity}}
  \\
  & \leq \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \left(\Ithree + \Ethree + \Pthree + \Kthree\right)
  && 
  \text{by \Cref{lemma:basic-algebra}}
  \\
  & = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 
  && \text{by \Cref{lemma:decomposition}}
  \\
  & = \tfrac{1}{2}.
\end{align*}
The next example is for the Ramsey multiplicity problem with $s = t = 3$, which asks us to find a tightest $c'$ that makes the following inequality valid for all limiting graphs:
\[I_3 + K_3 \geq c'.
\]
The tightest $c'$ is known to be $1/4$ by Goodman's result \hy{Citation}. We prove the validity of the above inequality for $c' = 1/4$ as follows:
\begin{align*}
  I_3 + K_3 
  & = 1 \cdot \Ithree + 0 \cdot \Ethree + 0 \cdot \Pthree + 1 \cdot \Kthree
  && \text{by \Cref{eqn:mantel-density-decomposition}}
  \\
  & \geq \left(1 \cdot \Ithree + 0 \cdot \Ethree + 0 \cdot \Pthree + 1 \cdot \Kthree\right)
  -
  \left(
  \tfrac{3}{4} \cdot \Ithree - \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \Ethree - \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \Pthree + \tfrac{3}{4} \cdot \Kthree
  \right)
  && \text{by \Cref{eqn:mantel-key-inequality}}
  \\
  & = \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \Ithree + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \Ethree + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \Pthree + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \Kthree
  && \text{by \Cref{lemma:basic-algebra}}
  \\
  & = \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot (\Ithree + \Ethree + \Pthree + \Kthree)
  && 
  \text{by \Cref{lemma:basic-algebra}}
  \\
  & = \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 1 
  && \text{by \Cref{lemma:decomposition}}
  \\
  & = \tfrac{1}{4}.
\end{align*}

\section{Reasoning with Downward Operator}

The previous section illustrated how to reason about limiting graphs using flag algebra with two examples. But both examples include a step not justified yet, namely, the inequality in \Cref{eqn:mantel-key-inequality} which is recalled below:
\[
0 \leq 1 \cdot \Ithree - \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Ethree - \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Pthree + 1 \cdot \Kthree.
\]
The purpose of this section is to explain a reasoning principle of flag algebra which allows us to prove such inequalities, and also downward operator, a key tool for applying this reasoning principle.

At a high level, the reasoning principle is based on the idea of defining a collection of variants of flag algebra and establishing, for each variant, a mechanism for transferring valid inequalities in the variant to valid inequalities in the original flag algebra. Each variant is designed to make certain inequalities easier to prove. An intended use case is to prove such easy inequalities in some variants, and transfer them to the original flag algebra using the mechanism. The variants we consider here are obtained by repeating the construction of flag algebra so far but with partially vertex-labelled graphs, instead of unlabelled graphs. For instance, when we derive the inequality in \Cref{eqn:mantel-key-inequality}, we use a variant that considers graphs with one vertex labelled with~$1$.

To express this principle formally, we need some additional definitions.
A \emph{type} $\tau$ is a graph on the vertex set $[k]$ for some $k \in \mathbb{N}$; we call $k$ the \emph{size} of $\tau$. A \emph{$\tau$-labelled graph} is a pair $G^\tau = (G, \theta)$ consisting of a graph $G$ and an injective map $\theta : [k] \to V(G)$ such that $\theta$ is an \emph{embedding} from $\tau$ into $G$, i.e., for every $i,j \in [k]$, we have $\{i,j\} \in E(\tau)$ if and only if $\{\theta(i), \theta(j)\} \in E(G)$. We call $\tau$ the \emph{type} of $G^\tau$, and write $\lvert G^\tau \rvert$ for $G$, and also $V(G^\tau)$ and $E(G^\tau)$ for $V(G)$ and $E(G)$, respectively. For a type $\tau$, we write $\mathcal{G}^\tau$ for the set of all $\tau$-labelled graphs.

We now repeat the concepts introduced in the previous sections but for $\tau$-labelled graphs instead of unlabelled graphs.
For $\tau$-labelled graphs $H^\tau = (H,\iota)$ and $G^\tau = (G,\theta)$, a function $h : V(H) \to V(G)$ is a \emph{$\tau$-homomorphism} if it is a homomorphism from $H$ to $G$ and satisfies $h(\iota(i)) = \theta(i)$ for all $i \in [k]$. We say that $H^\tau$ and $G^\tau$ are \emph{$\tau$-isomorphic}, denoted by $H^\tau \simeq_\tau G^\tau$, if there exists a bijective $\tau$-homomorphism from $H^\tau$ to $G^\tau$ such that its inverse is also a $\tau$-homomorphism from $G^\tau$ to $H^\tau$. 

For $\tau$-labelled graphs $H^\tau = (H,\iota)$ and $G^\tau = (G,\theta)$, the \emph{induced labelled-subgraph density} of $H^\tau$ in $G^\tau$, or simply \emph{$H^\tau$-density} in $G^\tau$, is defined as
\[
p_\tau(H^\tau,G^\tau) = \mathbb{P}\Big[\Big(G\big[\theta([k]) \cup \mathbf{U}\big],\,\theta\Big) \simeq_\tau H^\tau\Big]
\]
where $\mathbf{U}$ is a uniform random subset of $V(G) \setminus \theta([k])$ with size $|V(H^\tau)| - k$. A sequence of 
$\tau$-labelled graphs $(G^\tau_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is \emph{increasing} if $|V(G^\tau_n)| < |V(G^\tau_{n+1})|$. Such a sequence
\emph{converges} if it is increasing and $\lim_{n \to \infty} p_\tau(H^\tau, G^\tau_n)$ exists for every $\tau$-labelled graph $H^\tau$. A function $\phi : \mathcal{G}^\tau \to [0,1]$ is a \emph{$\tau$-labelled limiting graph} if there exists a convergent sequence of $\tau$-labelled graphs $(G^\tau_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ such that $\phi(H^\tau) = \lim_{n \to \infty} p_\tau(H^\tau, G^\tau_n)$ for every $\tau$-labelled graph $H^\tau$. As in the original flag algebra, $\phi$ represents an infinite undirected graph with a continuum number of vertices, but unlike the original case, the $k$ vertices of this infinite graph are assumed to be labelled with $1,\ldots,k$, and this label defines an embedding from $\tau$ into the infinite graph. The component of $\phi$ at a pattern $\tau$-labelled graph $H^\tau$ is the density of $H^\tau$ in this infinitely large host graph. We write $\Phi^\tau$ for the set of all $\tau$-labelled limiting graphs. \hy{Check that the above explanation is also included in the unlabelled case.}

The variant of flag algebra for $\tau$-labelled graphs is defined analogously to the original one, replacing graphs, induced subgraph densities, convergent graph sequences, and limiting graphs by $\tau$-labelled graphs, induced labelled-subgraph densities, convergent sequences of $\tau$-labelled graphs, and $\tau$-labelled limiting graphs, respectively. Its syntax consists of density expressions and assertions built from $\tau$-labelled graphs and is interpreted over $\tau$-labelled limiting graphs in $\Phi^\tau$. For convenience, the syntax is recalled below:
\begin{align*}
\text{Density Expressions}\quad E^\tau & ::= H^\tau \,\mid\, r \cdot E^\tau \,\mid\, 0 \,\mid\, E^\tau + E^\tau \,\mid\, 1 \,\mid\, E^\tau \cdot E^\tau
\\
\text{Assertions}\quad A^\tau & ::= \lfalse \,\mid\, \ltrue \,\mid\, E^\tau \geq E^\tau \,\mid\, \neg A^\tau \,\mid\, A^\tau \lor A^\tau 
\end{align*}
where $H^\tau$ is a $\tau$-labelled graph and $r \in \mathbb{R}$. We write $\Expr^\tau$ for the density expressions in this variant and $\Assn^\tau$ for its assertions.

All of \Cref{lemma:basic-algebra,lemma:isomorphism,lemma:non-negativity,lemma:sum-to-one,lemma:decomposition,lemma:eliminate-multiplication} hold in this $\tau$-labelled variant as well, with the obvious modifications replacing graphs by $\tau$-labelled graphs and induced subgraph densities by induced labelled-subgraph densities. We spell out one immediate consequence of \Cref{lemma:basic-algebra} and the labelled analogue of \Cref{lemma:eliminate-multiplication} for later use.
\begin{cor}
  \label{cor:basic-algebra-tau:sos}
  Let $\tau$ be a type with size $k$. Then, for all expressions $E_1^\tau,\ldots,E_m^\tau$ in $\Expr^\tau$,
  \[
    \Big(\sum_{i \in [m]} E_i^\tau \cdot E_i^\tau\Big) \geq 0
  \]
  is a valid assertion in the $\tau$-labelled variant of flag algebra.
\end{cor}
\begin{lem}
  \label{lemma:eliminate-multiplication-tau}
  Let $\tau$ be a type with size $k$, and $V$ a finite subset of $\mathbb{N}$ with $|V| \geq k$. Also, let $\mathcal{H}^\tau_V$ be a set of $\tau$-labelled graphs on $V$ such that every $\tau$-labelled graph is $\tau$-isomorphic to exactly one element of $\mathcal{H}^\tau_V$. Then, for every pair of $\tau$-labelled graphs $H_1^\tau$ and $H_2^\tau$ with $v(H_1^\tau) + v(H_2^\tau) - k \leq |V|$, there exist coefficients $r_{H^\tau}$ for each $H^\tau \in \mathcal{H}^\tau_V$ such that
  \[
    H_1^\tau \cdot H_2^\tau = \sum_{H^\tau \in \mathcal{H}^\tau_V} r_{H^\tau} \cdot H^\tau
  \]
  is a valid equation in the $\tau$-labelled variant of flag algebra. Also,
  \[
    1 = (\tau,\mathrm{id}_{[k]})
  \]
  is a valid equation in the $\tau$-labelled variant of flag algebra.
\end{lem}

The mechanism for transferring valid inequalities in the $\tau$-labelled variant to valid inequalities in the original flag algebra is called the \emph{downward operator}. It is defined by following a general recipe for relating the variant and the original flag algebras using what we call an adjoint pair of functions. We explain this recipe next and then instantiate it to define the downward operator. 

Consider a type $\tau$, and functions $\gamma : \mathcal{G} \to \FinMea(\mathcal{G}^\tau)$ and $\alpha : \mathcal{G}^\tau \to \FinMea(\mathcal{G})$. Here we regard $\mathcal{G}$ and $\mathcal{G}^\tau$ as measurable spaces with the discrete $\sigma$-algebras and write $\FinMea(\mathcal{G})$ and $\FinMea(\mathcal{G}^\tau)$ for the sets of finite measures on $\mathcal{G}$ and $\mathcal{G}^\tau$, respectively. Intuitively, for $G \in \mathcal{G}$, $\gamma(G)$ defines an unnormalised probability distribution over $\mathcal{G}^\tau$, and for $H^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau$, $\alpha(H^\tau)$ defines an unnormalised probability distribution over $\mathcal{G}$. We say that $(\alpha,\gamma)$ is an \emph{adjoint pair} if for all $H^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau$ and $G \in \mathcal{G}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:adjoint-pair}
p(\alpha(H^\tau), G) = p_\tau(H^\tau, \gamma(G)),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
  p(\alpha(H^\tau), G) \defeq \int_{\mathcal{G}} p(H, G) \, \Big(\alpha(H^\tau)(dH)\Big)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  p_\tau(H^\tau, \gamma(G)) \defeq \int_{\mathcal{G}^\tau} p_\tau(H^\tau, G^\tau) \, \Big(\gamma(G)(dG^\tau)\Big).
\end{align*}


The downward operator for a type $\tau$ is obtained from an adjoint pair $(\alpha,\gamma)$ for $\tau$ that satisfies two additional conditions. The first requires that $\gamma$ be extendable to limiting graphs; the second requires that $\alpha$ have finite support. We say that $\gamma$ is \emph{extendable to limiting graphs} if there exists a function $\gamma_\infty : \Phi \to \FinMea(\Phi^\tau)$ such that, for every convergent sequence of graphs $(G_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ and every limiting graph $\phi \in \Phi$,
\begin{multline*}
\Bigl(\forall H \in \mathcal{G},\ \lim_{n \to \infty} p(H, G_n) = \phi(H)\Bigr)
\\
\implies
\Bigl(\forall H^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau,\ \lim_{n \to \infty} p_\tau(H^\tau, \gamma(G_n)) = \int_{\Phi^\tau} \phi^\tau(H^\tau) \,\bigl(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\bigr)\Bigr).
\end{multline*}
Here, $\Phi^\tau$ is equipped with the $\sigma$-algebra induced by the product topology on $[0,1]^{\mathcal{G}^\tau}$, making it a measurable space. We say that $\alpha$ has \emph{finite support} if, for every $H^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau$, the support
\[
\support(\alpha(H^\tau)) \defeq \{ H \,:\ \alpha(H^\tau)(\{H\}) > 0 \}
\]
is finite. This finite-support condition lets us extend $\alpha$ linearly to any multiplication-free density expression in $\Expr^\tau$ that does not contain $1$:
\begin{align*}
  \alpha^\dagger(H^\tau) & \defeq \sum_{H \in \support(\alpha(H^\tau))} \alpha(H^\tau)(\{H\}) \cdot H,
  &
  \alpha^\dagger(r \cdot E^\tau) & \defeq r \cdot \alpha^\dagger(E^\tau),
  \\
  \alpha^\dagger(0) & \defeq 0,
  &
  \alpha^\dagger(E_1^\tau + E_2^\tau) & \defeq \alpha^\dagger(E_1^\tau) + \alpha^\dagger(E_2^\tau).
\end{align*}
\begin{lem}
  \label{lemma:adjoint-transfer}
  Let $(\alpha,\gamma)$ be an adjoint pair for a type $\tau$. Assume that $\gamma$ is extendable to limiting graphs. Also, assume that $\alpha(H^\tau)$
  has finite support, and let $\alpha^\dagger$ be the generalisation of $\alpha$ to multiplication-free expressions that do not contain $1$. Then, for every valid inequality $E^\tau \geq 0$ in the $\tau$-labelled variant such that $E^\tau$ is multiplication-free and $1$-free, the inequality $\alpha^\dagger(E^\tau) \geq 0$ is valid in the original flag algebra.
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
  Let $\gamma_\infty : \Phi \to \FinMea(\Phi^\tau)$ be a witness of the extendability 
  of $\gamma$ to limiting graphs.
  Consider an arbitrary limiting graph $\phi \in \Phi$. We need to show that $\db{\alpha^\dagger(E^\tau)}\phi \geq 0$. 
  Using structural induction on $E^\tau$, we will show that 
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lem:adjoint-transfer0}
    \db{\alpha^\dagger(E^\tau)}\phi = \int_{\Phi^\tau} \db{E^\tau}\phi^\tau \,\Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big).
  \end{equation}
  The desired conclusion then follows from this equation because $E^\tau \geq 0$ is valid in the $\tau$-labelled variant
  and so the integrand $\db{E^\tau}\phi^\tau$ is non-negative for every $\phi^\tau \in \Phi^\tau$.

  Assume that $E^\tau$ is a $\tau$-labelled graph $H^\tau$. By the definition of a limiting graph, there exists a convergent sequence of graphs $(G_n)_n$ such that 
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lem:adjoint-transfer1}
    \phi(H) = \lim_{n \to \infty} p(H, G_n)  \quad\text{for all } H \in \mathcal{G}.
  \end{equation}
  Using this, we derive \Cref{eq:lem:adjoint-transfer0} as follows:
  \begin{align*}
    & \db{\alpha^\dagger(H^\tau)}\phi 
    \\
    & \quad {} = \db{\sum_{H \in \support(\alpha(H^\tau))} \alpha(H^\tau)(\{H\}) \cdot H}\phi
    && \text{by the definition of } \alpha^\dagger
    \\
    & \quad {} = \sum_{H \in \support(\alpha(H^\tau))} \alpha(H^\tau)(\{H\}) \cdot \phi(H)
    && \text{by the definition of } \db{-}
    \\
    & \quad {} = \sum_{H \in \support(\alpha(H^\tau))} \alpha(H^\tau)(\{H\}) \cdot \lim_{n \to \infty} p(H, G_n)
    && \text{by \Cref{eq:lem:adjoint-transfer1}}
    \\
    & \quad {} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{H \in \support(\alpha(H^\tau))} \alpha(H^\tau)(\{H\}) \cdot p(H, G_n)
    && \text{by the finiteness of } \support(\alpha(H^\tau))
    \\
    & \quad {} = \lim_{n \to \infty} p(\alpha(H^\tau), G_n)
    && \text{by the definition of } p(\alpha(H^\tau), G_n)
    \\
    & \quad {} = \lim_{n \to \infty} p_\tau(H^\tau, \gamma(G_n))
    && \text{by the adjointness of } (\alpha,\gamma)
    \\
    & \quad {} = \int_{\Phi^\tau} \phi^\tau(H^\tau) \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    && \text{by the extendability of } \gamma,
    \\
    & \quad {} = \int_{\Phi^\tau} \db{H^\tau}\phi^\tau \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    && \text{by the definition of } \db{-}.
  \end{align*}

  Next consider the case that $E^\tau = 0$. Then, 
  \begin{align*}
    \db{\alpha^\dagger(0)}\phi 
    = \db{0}\phi
    = 0 
    = \int_{\Phi^\tau} 0 \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    = \int_{\Phi^\tau} \db{0}\phi^\tau \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big).
  \end{align*}

  The remaining cases can be proved by the induction hypothesis and the linearity of integration. We first derive the target equation for $E^\tau = r \cdot F^\tau$:
  \begin{align*}
    \db{\alpha^\dagger(r \cdot F^\tau)}\phi 
    & = \db{r \cdot \alpha^\dagger(F^\tau)}\phi
    && \text{by the definition of } \alpha^\dagger
    \\
    & = r \cdot \left(\db{\alpha^\dagger(F^\tau)}\phi\right)
    && \text{by the definition of } \db{-}
    \\
    & = r \cdot \int_{\Phi^\tau} \db{F^\tau}\phi^\tau \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    && \text{by the induction hypothesis}
    \\
    & = \int_{\Phi^\tau} r \cdot \left(\db{F^\tau}\phi^\tau\right) \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    && \text{by the linearity of integration}
    \\
    & = \int_{\Phi^\tau} \db{r \cdot F^\tau}\phi^\tau \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    && \text{by the definition of } \db{-}.
  \end{align*}
  Next, we handle the case that $E^\tau = E_1^\tau + E_2^\tau$:
  \begin{align*}
    & \db{\alpha^\dagger(E_1^\tau + E_2^\tau)}\phi 
    \\
    & \quad {} = \db{\alpha^\dagger(E_1^\tau) + \alpha^\dagger(E_2^\tau)}\phi
    && \text{by the definition of } \alpha^\dagger
    \\
    & \quad {} = \db{\alpha^\dagger(E_1^\tau)}\phi + \db{\alpha^\dagger(E_2^\tau)}\phi
    && \text{by the definition of } \db{-}
    \\
    & \quad {} = \int_{\Phi^\tau} \db{E_1^\tau}\phi^\tau \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    + \int_{\Phi^\tau} \db{E_2^\tau}\phi^\tau \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    && \text{by the induction hypothesis}
    \\
    & \quad {} = \int_{\Phi^\tau} \left(\db{E_1^\tau}\phi^\tau + \db{E_2^\tau}\phi^\tau\right) \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    && \text{by the linearity of integration}
    \\
    & \quad {} = \int_{\Phi^\tau} \db{E_1^\tau + E_2^\tau}\phi^\tau \, \Big(\gamma_\infty(\phi)(d\phi^\tau)\Big)
    && \text{by the definition of } \db{-}.     
  \end{align*}
\end{proof}

We are ready to define the downward operator for each type $\tau$. Given an expression $E^\tau$ in $\Expr^\tau$, we first eliminate all multiplications and occurrences of the constant $1$ in $E^\tau$ using \Cref{lemma:eliminate-multiplication-tau}, obtaining a multiplication-free, $1$-free expression $F^\tau$ that is equal to $E^\tau$ in the $\tau$-labelled variant of flag algebra. Although this elimination step is underspecified, it is typically performed from the innermost subexpression outward. The downward operator then maps $F^\tau$ to $\alpha_d^\dagger(F^\tau)$, where $\alpha_d$ is part of a specific adjoint pair $(\alpha_d,\gamma_d)$ for the type $\tau$ defined below. This adjoint pair satisfies the conditions in \Cref{lemma:adjoint-transfer}. Consequently, if $E^\tau \geq 0$ is a valid inequality in the $\tau$-labelled variant, then $\alpha_d^\dagger(F^\tau) \geq 0$ is valid in the original flag algebra.

To define the adjoint pair $(\alpha_d,\gamma_d)$ mentioned above, consider a $\tau$-labelled graph $H^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau$ and an unlabelled graph $G \in \mathcal{G}$. For each $G^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau$, let $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{G^\tau}$ be the uniform random injection from $[k]$ to $V(G^\tau)$, and also let
\[
  q_{G^\tau} \defeq \mathbb{P}\Big[\boldsymbol{\theta}_{G^\tau} \text{ is an embedding from $\tau$ to $|G^\tau|$ and $(|G^\tau|,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{G^\tau}) \simeq_\tau G^\tau$}\Big].
\]
Note that $(\tau,\mathrm{id}_{[k]})$ is a $\tau$-labelled graph whose underlying graph is $\tau$ itself, 
and that $q_{(\tau,\mathrm{id})} = |\mathrm{Aut}(\tau)| / v(\tau)!$ where $\mathrm{Aut}(\tau)$ is the set of all automorphisms on $\tau$. Using this notation,
we define finite measures $\alpha_d(H^\tau) \in \FinMea(\mathcal{G})$ and $\gamma_d(G) \in \FinMea(\mathcal{G}^\tau)$ as follows:
\begin{align*}
  \alpha_d(H^\tau)(\{H\}) & \defeq 
  \begin{cases}
  q_{H^\tau} & \text{if } |H^\tau| = H
  \\
  0 & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \\
  \gamma_d(G)(\{G^\tau\}) & \defeq 
  \begin{cases}
  \big(q_{(\tau,\mathrm{id})} \cdot p(\tau,G)\big) \div \big|\{G^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau \,:\ |G^\tau| = G\}\big| & \text{if } |G^\tau| = G
  \\
  0 & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
\end{align*}
Among the conditions in \Cref{lemma:adjoint-transfer}, it is straightforward to see that $\alpha_d(H^\tau)$ has finite support for every $H^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau$. Also, it can be shown that $\gamma_d$ is extendable to limiting graphs, although the proof is involved. See \hy{Results in Razborov's paper} for details. Finally, the following lemma establishes the adjointness of $(\alpha_d,\gamma_d)$.
\begin{lem}
  \label{lemma:downward-adjoint}
  The pair $(\alpha_d,\gamma_d)$ defined above is an adjoint pair for the type $\tau$.
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
  \hy{Say how the proof here is related to Razborov's proof.}
  Consider an arbitrary $\tau$-labelled graph $H^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau$ and an arbitrary unlabelled graph $G \in \mathcal{G}$. We need to show that
  \[
    p(\alpha_d(H^\tau), G) = p_\tau(H^\tau, \gamma_d(G)).
  \]
  We start by simplifying the left-hand side of the above equation:
  \begin{align*}
    p(\alpha_d(H^\tau), G)
    & = \int_{\mathcal{G}} p(H, G) \, \Big(\alpha_d(H^\tau)(dH)\Big)
    \\
    & = q_{H^\tau} \cdot p(|H^\tau|, G)
    && \text{by the definition of } \alpha_d(H^\tau).
  \end{align*}
  Let $Z_G \defeq \big|\{G^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau \,:\ |G^\tau| = G\}\big|$.
  If there does not exist a $\tau$-labelled graph $G^\tau$ with $|G^\tau| = G$, the right-hand side of the target equation is zero by the definition of $\gamma_d(G)$. In this case, we also have that $p(|H^\tau|, G) = 0$; otherwise, there would be a subset $V_0$ of $V(G)$ such that $\tau$ is isomorphic to $G[V_0]$ and the pair of $G$ and this ismorphism forms $G^\tau$ with $|G^\tau| = G$, which contradicts to the assumption that no such $G^\tau$ exists. Thus, in the case, the left-hand side of the target equation is also zero, and the equation follows.
  
  Now assume that there exists a $\tau$-labelled graph $G^\tau$ with $|G^\tau| = G$. Under this assumption, we simplify the right-hand side of the target equation as follows:
  \begin{align*}
    p_\tau(H^\tau, \gamma_d(G))
    & = \int_{\mathcal{G}^\tau} p_\tau(H^\tau, G^\tau) \, \Big(\gamma_d(G)(dG^\tau)\Big)
    \\
    & = \sum_{\substack{G^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau \\ |G^\tau| = G}} p_\tau(H^\tau, G^\tau) \cdot 
    \big(q_{(\tau,\mathrm{id})} \cdot p(\tau,G)\big) \div Z_G
    && \text{by the definition of } \gamma_d(G)
    \\
    & = \frac{q_{(\tau ,\mathrm{id})} \cdot p(\tau,G)}{Z_G} \cdot \sum_{\substack{G^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau \\ |G^\tau| = G}} p_\tau(H^\tau, G^\tau).
  \end{align*}
  To complete the proof, it suffices to show that
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:downward-adjoint:target-eq}
    q_{H^\tau} \cdot p(|H^\tau|, G) = \Big(q_{(\tau ,\mathrm{id})} \cdot p(\tau,G)\Big) \cdot \sum_{\substack{G^\tau \in \mathcal{G}^\tau \\ |G^\tau| = G}} \frac{1}{Z_G} \cdot p_\tau(H^\tau, G^\tau).
  \end{equation}

  Let $k = v(\tau)$, $m = v(H^\tau)$, and $n = v(G)$. Define $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ as a uniform random injection from $[k]$ to $V(H^\tau)$, and  $\boldsymbol{\theta}'$ as a uniform random injection from $[k]$ to $V(G)$. Also, let $\mathbf{U}$ be a uniform random subset of $V(G)$ of size $m$, and $\mathbf{U}'$ be a uniform random subset of $V(G) \setminus \boldsymbol{\theta}'([k])$ of size $m-k$. Using these random variables, we define two events $E$ and $E'$ as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $E$ is the event that $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is an embedding of $\tau$ into $|H^\tau|$, the $\tau$-labelled graph $(|H^\tau|, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ is $\tau$-isomorphic to $H^\tau$, and the induced subgraph $G[\mathbf{U}]$ is isomorphic to $|H^\tau|$.
      \item $E'$ is the event that $\boldsymbol{\theta}'$ is an embedding of $\tau$ into $G$, and the $\tau$-labelled subgraph induced by $\mathbf{U}' \cup \boldsymbol{\theta}'([k])$ and labelled by $\boldsymbol{\theta}'$, denoted as $(G[\mathbf{U}' \cup \boldsymbol{\theta}'([k])],\boldsymbol{\theta}')$, is $\tau$-isomorphic to $H^\tau$.
    \end{itemize}

  The left-hand side of \Cref{eq:downward-adjoint:target-eq} is equal to the probability of $E$. The random variables $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ are independent, and $q_{H^\tau}$ and $p(|H^\tau|,G)$ are the probabilities that $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ satistfy their respective conditions in the event $E$. Similarly, the right-hand side of \Cref{eq:downward-adjoint:target-eq} is equal to the probability of $E'$. The factor $q_{(\tau ,\mathrm{id})} \cdot p(\tau,G)$ in the right-hand side is the probability of the event $E''$ that $\boldsymbol{\theta}'$ is an embedding of $\tau$ into $G$, and the remaining factor equals the conditional probability $\mathbb{P}[E' \mid E'']$.  
  
  Thus, it suffices to show that $\mathbb{P}[E] = \mathbb{P}[E']$. We note that the joint distribution of $(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{U})$ is the uniform distribution over the set of all pairs $(\theta, U)$ where $\theta$ is an injection from $[k]$ to $V(H^\tau)$ and $U$ is a subset of $V(G)$ with size $m$. Similarly, the joint distribution of $(\boldsymbol{\theta}', \mathbf{U}')$ is the uniform distribution over the set of all pairs $(\theta', U')$ where $\theta'$ is an injection from $[k]$ to $V(G)$ and $U'$ is a subset of $V(G) \setminus \theta'([k])$ with size $m-k$. Elementary counting confirms that these two sample spaces have the same cardinality $n! / ((n-m)! (m-k)!)$. As a result, for such $(U,\theta)$ and $(U',\theta')$, we have
  \[
  \mathbb{P}\big[(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{U}) = (\theta, U)\big] = \mathbb{P}\big[(\boldsymbol{\theta}',\mathbf{U}') = (\theta', U')\big] = \frac{(n-m)! (m-k)!}{n!}.
  \]
  Therefore, to show that $\mathbb{P}[E] = \mathbb{P}[E']$, we just need to construct a bijection between the set of those $(\theta, U)$ satisfying $E$ and the set of those $(\theta', U')$ satisfying $E'$. For each 
  $U_0 \subseteq V(G)$ with size $m$ such that $G[U_0]$ is isomorphic to $|H^\tau|$, we fix one isomorphism $f_{U_0} : V(H^\tau) \to U_0$ from $|H^\tau|$ to $G[U_0]$. Using this, we construct the desired bijection as follows.
  Given a pair $(\theta, U)$ satisfying $E$, we define the corresponding pair $(\theta', U')$ satisfying $E'$ as follows. By the definition of $E$, $G[U]$ is isomorphic to $|H^\tau|$. So, we have the isomorphism $f_U$ 
  from $|H^\tau|$ to $G[U]$. We define $\theta' : [k] \to V(G)$ by $\theta' \defeq f_U \circ \theta$, and also define $U' \defeq U \setminus \theta'([k])$. Then, $(\theta', U')$ satisfies the event $E'$. Furthermore, this construction is invertible: given a pair $(\theta', U')$ satisfying $E'$, we can recover the corresponding pair $(\theta, U)$ satisfying $E$ by letting $U \defeq U' \cup \theta'([k])$ and $\theta \defeq f_U^{-1} \circ \theta'$. This completes the proof of \Cref{eq:downward-adjoint:target-eq} and thus of the lemma.
\end{proof}

We illustrate the downward operator by deriving \Cref{eqn:mantel-key-inequality} with it. Consider the type $\tau$ with size $1$. That is, $\tau$ is the graph with a single vertex and no edges. By \Cref{cor:basic-algebra-tau:sos}, we have
\[
\left(\LItwo - \LEtwo\right) \cdot \left(\LItwo - \LEtwo\right) \geq 0
\]
in the $\tau$-labelled variant of flag algebra. Then, we eliminate the multiplication using \Cref{lemma:eliminate-multiplication-tau}:
\begin{align*}
\left(\LItwo - \LEtwo\right) \cdot \left(\LItwo - \LEtwo\right) 
& {} = \LItwo \cdot \LItwo - 2 \cdot \left(\LItwo \cdot \LEtwo\right) + \LEtwo \cdot \LEtwo
\\
& {} = \left(\LIthree + \LEthreeC\right) - 2 \cdot \left(\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \LEthreeB + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \LPthreeB\right) + 
\left(\LPthreeC + \LKthree\right)
\\
& {} = \LIthree + \LEthreeC - \LEthreeB - \LPthreeB + \LPthreeC + \LKthree.
\end{align*}
Thus, by \Cref{lemma:adjoint-transfer}, we have
\[
\alpha^\dagger_d\left(\LIthree + \LEthreeC - \LEthreeB - \LPthreeB + \LPthreeC + \LKthree\right) \geq 0
\]
in the original flag algebra. We now compute the expression inside the downward operator and derive \Cref{eqn:mantel-key-inequality}.
\begin{align*}
\alpha^\dagger_d\left(\LIthree + \LEthreeC - \LEthreeB - \LPthreeB + \LPthreeC + \LKthree\right)
& =\alpha_d\left(\LIthree\right) + \alpha_d\left(\LEthreeC\right) - \alpha_d\left(\LEthreeB\right) - \alpha_d\left(\LPthreeB\right) + \alpha_d\left(\LPthreeC\right) + \alpha_d\left(\LKthree\right)
\\
& = \Ithree + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Ethree - \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \Ethree - \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \Pthree + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Pthree + \Kthree
\\
& = \Ithree - \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \Ethree - \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \Pthree + \Kthree.
\end{align*}
